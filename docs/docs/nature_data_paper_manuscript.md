## Abstract

Air permits issued under Title V of the U.S. Clean Air Act describe each facility's emission sources, control equipment, pollutant limits, and the rules that apply to it. Researchers rarely use this information, because it sits in thousands of PDF documents of varying format on dozens of state and federal agency websites. We present a dataset of emission-unit records extracted from more than 118,000 publicly available air permit documents, collected from EPA and state agency portals across all 50 U.S. states and the District of Columbia. The current release holds 1,621,542 records from 74,017 unique permit documents. Each record has 52 standard fields that identify the facility and permit and describe individual pieces of equipment: capacity, fuel type, emission limits by pollutant, control devices, opacity and throughput limits, rated efficiency, and the federal subparts that apply. Matched against the EPA ECHO list of active major sources, the dataset covers 66.2% of the 13,546 facilities that hold Title V permits nationwide. An open-source pipeline produces the records. It downloads permits from public portals, extracts their text (with OCR for scanned documents), and uses large language models (LLMs) to pull structured fields from the text. Every record names the model that produced it and links to its source PDF through a stable filename key, so anyone can check it against the original. We describe how the data were collected, the record schema, how we validated the extraction, and the known limitations. The dataset supports facility-level environmental research, assessments of emissions technology, regulatory analysis, and environmental justice studies at a national scale that manual permit review could not reach.

## 1. Background and Summary

### 1.1 The Title V Operating Permit Program

Title V of the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments (42 U.S.C. sections 7661--7661f) created an operating permit program for major sources of air pollution in the United States. A facility must hold a Title V permit if its emissions exceed set thresholds: generally 100 tons per year of any criteria pollutant, 10 tons per year of a single hazardous air pollutant (HAP), or 25 tons per year of all HAPs combined. The permits are federally enforceable. State, local, tribal, and territorial agencies (together, "permitting authorities") issue them, or the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) does where no approved state program exists.

A Title V permit is a complete compliance document. It gathers every federal and state air quality requirement that applies to the facility, along with its emission limits, monitoring, recordkeeping, and reporting duties, into one instrument. Permits are usually renewed every five years. Each one describes the facility and its individual emission sources (emission units): equipment descriptions, rated capacities, fuel types, emission limits by pollutant, opacity and visible emission limits, throughput and production caps, pollution control equipment, and the federal standards that apply, such as the National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants (NESHAP) and New Source Performance Standards (NSPS). There are an estimated 10,000 or more active Title V permits nationwide. That makes the program one of the richest sources of facility-level environmental data in the United States, and also one of the most scattered.

### 1.2 The Data Access Problem

Title V permits are public, but they are hard to analyze in bulk. They are spread across more than 50 permitting authority websites. Each site has its own layout, file naming, and access method, from static HTML lists to JavaScript applications. The documents are PDFs of mixed quality: some are born digital with extractable text, and others are scanned images that need optical character recognition (OCR). No jurisdiction uses a standard format, and within one state the layout can change by region, year, or permit writer. A researcher who wants to study emission sources, control technologies, or regulatory stringency nationally must therefore find, download, read, and transcribe hundreds or thousands of documents by hand.

Earlier compilations of facility-level air quality data have drawn on self-reported emissions inventories, such as the EPA's National Emissions Inventory (NEI) [3] and the Toxics Release Inventory (TRI) [4], or on permit registries such as the Enforcement and Compliance History Online (ECHO) database [5]. A few sector databases go down to the equipment level for narrow source types. The Emissions & Generation Resource Integrated Database (eGRID) [6] and the Clean Air Markets Program Data [7] do so for electric generating units, and the Facility Registry Service (FRS) [8] supplies authoritative facility identifiers across EPA programs. These sources are valuable, but for the broader set of industrial sources they lack the equipment-level detail that permits contain: the capacity of each emission unit, its emission limits, its control devices, its fuel, its throughput restrictions, and the federal subparts that apply to it. This gap between what permits record and what structured databases offer limits research on the adoption of emissions technology, the effectiveness of regulation, the performance of control equipment, and cumulative impacts at the facility level, including environmental justice studies that need to know what equipment runs where [9].

Recent work has shown that large language models can extract accurate structured records from varied scientific and technical documents at scale [10,11]. For a corpus like air permits, whose formats vary too much for rule-based parsers, this offers a practical alternative to hand-built extraction code. This dataset applies that approach to Title V permits.

### 1.3 Dataset Overview

We present a structured dataset of emission-unit records extracted from public U.S. air permit documents. An open-source, reproducible pipeline produces it. The pipeline downloads permits, extracts their text, and uses large language models (LLMs) to extract the fields. The current release draws on a corpus of more than 118,000 permit PDFs from EPA and state agency portals across all 50 states and the District of Columbia. Of these, 74,017 unique permit documents have been processed so far, yielding 1,621,542 structured records with 52 standard fields.

Each record describes one emission unit found in a permit, or the permit as a whole when no individual units are found. Its fields fall into three groups: (1) facility and permit identification (facility name, address, geographic identifiers, industry classification, permit number, issuance and expiration dates, regulatory authority, and primary applicable regulations); (2) emission unit attributes (unit identifier, description, equipment type, manufacturer, model, year of manufacture, capacity, fuel type, pollutants, emission limits, control devices, rated efficiency, annual run hours, and generation capacity); and (3) processing provenance (source filename, extraction status, processing date, and model used). Every record links to its source PDF through a stable filename key, so it can be checked and audited independently.

The pipeline and dataset are built to extend to more jurisdictions and permit programs. All acquisition, extraction, and post-processing code is released in an open-source repository, with documented entry points and environment specifications so that the results can be reproduced.

## 2. Methods

The pipeline has four stages: (1) downloading permit PDFs from public portals, (2) extracting text from the PDFs, with OCR as a fallback, (3) extracting structured fields with an LLM, and (4) post-processing and merging everything into one table. The code repository holds the full implementation. We describe each stage in enough detail to reproduce it and leave the low-level details to the repository documentation.

### 2.1 Data Collection

The source documents are Title V operating permits in PDF form, downloaded from public agency portals. Portals differ widely, from static HTML pages to JavaScript single-page applications and from flat file lists to nested document repositories, so we collect documents in three complementary ways.

**Agentic portal crawling.** The repository includes a general-purpose "agentic" downloader that explores an unfamiliar portal from a set of starting URLs. It renders pages with Selenium, collects candidate links (giving priority to links in tables, which often hold permit listings), and picks out files that look like permits or PDFs. For ambiguous links it can ask an LLM to score the link text and surrounding page for relevance, then follows the high-confidence links to a set depth within the same domain. Downloads first try a direct HTTP fetch with retries and session cookies. If direct access is blocked, or the link goes through an intermediate landing page, the downloader falls back to driving the browser.

**State-specific download scripts.** For portals with a consistent layout and many documents, the repository provides scripts written for that portal. They handle its table layout, pagination, and link formats. This cuts false positives and improves coverage where the yield is highest. Examples include scripts for Virginia DEQ, Connecticut DEEP, Wyoming DEQ, Ohio EPA, Iowa DNR, and other states. A second set of scripts targets a type of facility rather than a portal. Data-center permits are dominated by large fleets of on-site generators, so for Texas, Georgia, Oregon, Nevada, Arizona, Mississippi, Tennessee, Iowa, Illinois, North Carolina, Ohio, Wisconsin, Washington, and Maryland we look up construction and operating permits facility by facility in the state agency's document index. These data-center lanes contribute 4,811 documents to the release and are the main source of the non-Title V permits described in Section 5.3.

**EPA permit hub.** The EPA's central permit hub is a JavaScript application, which we drive with Selenium. The downloader opens each permit page, finds the "Permitting Authority Documents" table, picks the "Final Permit" row, and downloads the PDF. It records finished links so later runs only fetch new documents.

All downloaded PDFs go into a structured folder tree under the project's data directory. The extraction pipeline lists PDFs from every source and skips files it has already processed.

### 2.2 Text Extraction

Each PDF is converted to a single plain-text file for the LLM to read.

**Native extraction.** We first read the text with the PyPDF2 library [12], page by page, keeping page-break markers. If the result is not empty and averages at least 30 characters per page, we use it as is.

**OCR fallback.** If native extraction fails, returns no text, or falls below that threshold, the pipeline switches to optical character recognition. It renders the pages to images at 200 DPI with Poppler (through pdf2image) and reads them with Tesseract [13], five pages at a time to limit memory use. Together the two paths handle both born-digital and scanned permits.

Each PDF yields one text file in an intermediate directory. The filename, without its extension, is the stable key that ties the PDF, its text, and its records together. Files that already exist or have been processed are skipped, and failed extractions are logged.

### 2.3 Information Extraction

A single LLM call extracts the structured data from each text file. The prompt asks for a JSON object with the general permit fields and an array of emission unit objects, each with the full set of unit fields. The prompt template is in the code repository and is archived with the dataset. It spells out field formats (ISO 8601 dates, semicolon-separated lists of pollutants and limits, standard fuel type names), explains how to tell similar things apart (facility address versus corporate address, facility operating hours versus unit run hours, emission point versus emission unit identifiers), and gives a complete example of the expected JSON. The model is told to use null for missing values and to extract only what the permit states or clearly implies.

**Models.** Extraction calls go through two OpenAI-compatible interfaces: an institutional API gateway (CBORG, at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory) for the earlier campaigns, and Google Vertex AI batch prediction for the campaigns run from July to September 2026. Each record names the model that produced it in the `Model Used` field and the campaign in `Source Run`. In the current release, Gemini 2.5 Flash [15] produced 79% of records, gemma-4-thinking 9%, Llama [NOTE: confirm exact version behind the `lbl/llama` alias] 4%, and gpt-oss-120b [14] 1%. Another 6% come from early campaigns that did not record the model string, and 0.3% carry a gateway alias (`gemini-flash`) used for the August data-center lane [NOTE: confirm the Gemini version behind the alias]. The later campaigns request structured output with an enforced response schema. This removes the JSON parsing failures that caused most extraction errors in earlier runs. [NOTE: the Section 4 validation sample was drawn from the August 2026 snapshot; see editorial note 4.]

**Schema.** General permit fields include: Facility Name, Owner/Operator Name, Facility Address, City, State Abbreviation, Zip Code, County, NAICS Code, SIC Code, Operating Hours, Industry Description, Permit Number, Permit Type, Issuance Date, Expiration Date, Regulatory Authority, and Primary Applicable Regulations. Emission unit fields include: Unit ID, Unit Description, Unit Quantity, Unit Make, Unit Model, Year of Manufacture, Unit Type, Pollutants, Emission Limits, Opacity Limit, Throughput/Production Limit, Control Device(s), Applicable NESHAP/NSPS Subpart, Capacity Value, Capacity Unit, Fuel Type, Rated Efficiency, Annual Run Hours, and Generation Capacity. Four more fields (Operating Hours Value, Operating Hours Time Basis, Annual Run Hours Value, Annual Run Hours Time Basis) are parsed from the raw text fields with pattern matching after extraction. Two further columns (NAICS Resolved, NAICS Source) come from the industry-classification step described in Section 5.2, not from the extraction call.

**Row construction.** Each emission unit in the JSON array becomes one row, and the permit's general fields are copied onto every row for that permit. If no units are found, the permit gets a single row with the unit fields set to null. Every row carries the source filename, processing status, processing date, and model identifier.

**Failure handling.** When extraction fails (malformed JSON, an API error, or a text reading failure), the document still gets a row, with a status code that says what went wrong and sentinel values in the content fields. Every processed file therefore appears in the output, and failure rates can be read directly from it.

**Large document handling.** Long permits were at first the pipeline's main source of error. A permit of several hundred thousand characters fits easily inside a million-token context window, so early extraction calls returned without error but quietly listed only a fraction of the emission units present. The median number of units per 100,000 characters fell from 7--8 in documents under 150,000 characters to 1.4 in documents over 700,000. The problem was on the output side, not the input side: the model stopped listing units long before it ran out of document.

The pipeline therefore splits long documents into chunks whether or not they would fit the context window. Chunks are at most 80,000 characters. Any chunk whose output is cut off is split in half and retried, recursively. The chunk results are then merged, with duplicates removed by unit identifier and the most complete version of each unit kept. Every document in the corpus above the length threshold was re-extracted this way. Merges only add: a re-extraction can add units or fill in fields on existing ones, but it never removes a unit recorded by an earlier run, because validation showed that any single pass misses a small tail of units that other passes catch. The effect was large. Total unit records roughly quadrupled, and individual long permits went from single-digit unit counts to hundreds (one 1.5-million-character refinery permit yielded 719 units where it had yielded 9). Documents in which one or more chunks failed even after retries are flagged with a non-zero `Pieces Failed` value (2,333 documents, 3.2% of released documents) so that users can exclude or re-process them.

### 2.4 Post-Processing and Consolidation

All extracted rows are combined into one table. Post-processing then: (1) parses the free-text operating hours and annual run hours fields into separate numeric value and time basis columns; (2) standardizes fuel type strings (for example, "nat gas" becomes "natural gas" and "#2 oil" becomes "#2 fuel oil"); (3) standardizes ZIP codes to five or nine digits and dates to ISO 8601; (4) fills an empty capacity field from the unit description when the description contains a recognizable value (MMBtu/hr, MW, hp, cfm, tons/day, lb/hr); and (5) deduplicates at the facility level. For step 5, when several permit documents describe the same facility (matched on standardized facility name, address, city, state, and ZIP code), the most recently processed extraction is kept, the number of contributing documents is written to `Duplicate Equipment Documents`, and the kept document is named in `Latest Facility Filename`. The combined output is written as an Excel file with columns in an order that suits analysts: processing provenance, facility identification, emission unit attributes with their parsed subfields, permit identifiers, and file metadata.

**Reproducibility.** A given dataset version can be rebuilt from the repository commit or release tag, the set of source PDFs (or a documented manifest of them), and the runtime environment (Python packages, OCR tools, and LLM API configuration). The repository README gives the exact commands, paths, and environment requirements.

## 3. Data Records

The dataset is distributed as a single table in both CSV and Excel formats, archived with a persistent identifier [DOI placeholder: Zenodo deposit, to be minted at submission]. Per-state subsets and a manifest are provided for convenience.

### 3.1 Record Structure

Each record is one emission unit from a permit, or one permit-level record when no individual units were found. Permit-level fields are repeated on every unit row for that permit, so the table can be filtered and aggregated without joins. The `Filename` column is the key that links each row to its source PDF and intermediate text file.

### 3.2 Schema Overview

The dataset has 52 columns in four groups:

**Processing and provenance fields** (6 columns): `Status`, `Processing Date`, `Model Used`, `Filename`, `Source Run`, and `Pieces Failed`. These say which document produced the row, whether extraction succeeded, which model produced it, which campaign it belongs to, and whether any chunk of a long document failed. Status values include "Success," "Success (No Units Found)," "LLM Extraction Failed," and "Text Reading Failed."

**General permit fields** (22 columns): facility identification (Facility Name, Owner/Operator Name, Facility Address, Facility City, Facility State Abbreviation, Facility Zip Code, Facility County), industry classification (NAICS Code, SIC Code, Classified NAICS, and the resolved pair NAICS Resolved and NAICS Source described in Section 5.2), operating context (Operating Hours with its parsed value and time-basis subfields, Industry Description), and permit identification (Permit Number, Permit Type, Issuance Date, Expiration Date, Regulatory Authority, Primary Applicable Regulations).

**Emission unit fields** (21 columns): unit identification (Unit ID, Unit Description, Unit Quantity, Unit Type), equipment attributes (Unit Make, Unit Model, Year of Manufacture, Capacity Value, Capacity Unit, Fuel Type, Rated Efficiency, Generation Capacity), operating parameters (Annual Run Hours with its parsed value and time-basis subfields), and regulatory content (Pollutants, Emission Limits, Opacity Limit, Throughput/Production Limit, Control Device(s), Applicable NESHAP/NSPS Subpart).

**File metadata fields** (3 columns): Spec Sheet Link, Duplicate Equipment Documents, and Latest Facility Filename, used for deduplication and cross-referencing.

How often a field is filled depends on how consistently permits state it. Industry classification is covered in Section 5.2, which describes the resolved code and where it comes from; the figures here are for the raw extracted fields. Among successful records, Unit ID is filled in 92% of rows, Permit Type in 88%, Owner/Operator Name in 81%, Issuance Date in 79%, Pollutants in 69%, SIC Code in 57%, Capacity Value in 55%, Emission Limits in 51%, Control Device(s) in 43%, Applicable NESHAP/NSPS Subpart in 33%, Throughput/Production Limit in 19%, and Opacity Limit in 15%. The sparse fields are sparse because permits do not state them, not because extraction missed them. Opacity and throughput limits, for example, apply only to certain kinds of equipment.

### 3.4 Representativeness against external benchmarks

We collected permits from whichever public repositories we could reach, not from a defined sampling frame. To show how representative the result is, we compare it with two outside references. The EPA ECHO list of active major (Title V) air facilities defines the target population. U.S. Census economic surveys weight that population by industrial activity.

We matched dataset facilities to ECHO facilities within each state by standardized facility name. An exact match on the standardized name is accepted first. Otherwise a fuzzy match at a conservative token-set threshold is accepted, but only if the two names share at least two distinctive words (generic words such as "plant," "station," or "compressor" do not count) or one word that is rare in that state's ECHO list. This rule stops a name like "Compressor Station 1" from matching every compressor station in a state. Each document contributes the facility name read from the permit and, where the source agency's document index gives one (13,289 documents), the agency's registry name for the facility. The registry name matters because permits often print a plant or unit name where ECHO records the registry name; 671 facilities, 415 of them in Texas, match only through the registry name. Coverage is the share of ECHO major facilities matched by at least one dataset facility.

Nationally, the dataset covers 66.2% of the 13,546 active major air facilities in ECHO. The benchmark spans the 52 jurisdictions that hold ECHO major sources: the 50 states, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico. Coverage across them is still uneven, but it no longer splits into two clusters: 27 jurisdictions holding 9,657 majors are well covered (at least 70%), 16 holding 2,700 majors are partly covered (30--70%), and 9 holding 1,189 majors remain sparse (below 30%). The sparse group consists almost entirely of jurisdictions that publish permits only through one-facility-at-a-time search portals, bot-protected applications, or records requests, with no bulk repository. Four jurisdictions have no matched facilities at all: Kansas (243 majors), Nebraska (122), Delaware (45), and the District of Columbia (43). Puerto Rico is effectively uncovered as well (1 of 44 majors), since the corpus holds a single document from it. The acquisition figures elsewhere in this paper describe the 50 states and the District of Columbia, where we collected at scale.

To test whether these gaps follow economic importance or only repository access, we weighted state coverage by manufacturing activity from the Census Annual Survey of Manufactures (ASM, 2021) [19] and County Business Patterns (CBP, 2022) [20]. ASM reports value added, shipments, and employment for manufacturing (NAICS 31--33) by state, which measures economic size in a way that establishment counts do not. Weighting each state's coverage by its manufacturing value added gives national coverage of 69.1%, and weighting by manufacturing employment gives 68.7%. Both are above the plain average of state coverages (59.6%), so the dataset covers the larger manufacturing states better than the smaller ones (Spearman +0.36 for value added, +0.31 for employment). At the county level, CBP establishment counts show almost no relationship between coverage and local manufacturing intensity: coverage is 60.6%, 65.4%, 67.4%, and 67.7% from the least to the most manufacturing-intensive quartile, and the rank correlation is negligible (Spearman 0.02--0.04). Coverage is also even across industrial sectors. A sector's representation ratio is its share of matched facilities divided by its share of the ECHO list. Every sector with more than 5% of the ECHO list falls between 0.85 and 1.10: manufacturing (1.10), utilities (0.99), transportation (0.94), administrative and waste services (0.88), and mining and oil and gas (0.85). Taken together, these results say that the remaining gaps come mainly from where accessible repositories happen to be, not from any systematic over- or under-representation of economically important or sector-specific activity.

Targeted collection closes these gaps. The states that an earlier snapshot of this dataset identified as the largest economic blind spots have since been collected through newly built repository interfaces, and they now rank among the better-covered jurisdictions. Louisiana went from no coverage to 94% of its 475 majors, Wisconsin from near zero to 78%, Texas (the second-largest manufacturing state, with 9.8% of national manufacturing value added) from 6% to 71%, and Ohio from 38% to 76%. Mining and oil and gas, previously the most under-represented sector at a representation ratio of 0.50, rose to 0.85 as a direct result of the Texas and Louisiana collections.

The largest remaining gaps are smaller in economic terms and sit in jurisdictions whose permits cannot be downloaded in bulk: New Jersey (9% covered, 1.8% of national manufacturing value added), Oregon (30%, 1.3%), Kansas (0%, 1.3%), Arizona (16%, 1.3%), Utah (39%, 1.1%), Oklahoma (22%, 1.0%), and Nebraska (0%, 1.0%). Each reflects a specific access barrier rather than a choice on our part: bot-protected portals, document systems that require an account, permits spread across county programs, and agencies that publish no final permits online. The representativeness figures here describe coverage at one point in time and should improve as more repositories are collected. A script in the repository rebuilds the whole benchmark from the ECHO, ASM, and CBP sources.

### 3.5 Missingness and Status Conventions

Missing values are null or empty cells. When extraction fails for a permit, the dataset keeps a row for it with the matching status code and null or sentinel values in the content fields. This lets users measure coverage, failure rates, and extraction quality across the whole dataset. Filter on the `Status` column as described in the Usage Notes.

## 4. Technical Validation

Technical validation measures extraction quality and describes the kinds of errors that arise from varied document formats, OCR artifacts, and how the LLM reads ambiguous permit language.

[NOTE: the earlier panels in Section 4.2 were executed against snapshots in which `lbl/llama` produced most records; they are retained as historical robustness checks. The August 2026 panel reported at the end of Section 4.2 is the one that validates the current release---it samples the released dataset itself, stratified to its actual model composition. The document-grounded audit in Section 4.1 uses the same sample. The sample was drawn from the 56,749-document snapshot of 2026-08-02; the 17,268 documents (23% of the release) and 512,421 records (32%) added since were all produced by the Gemini 2.5 Flash chunked configuration that forms the largest stratum of the sample (Section 2.3), so the panel's per-model results apply to them, but they were not themselves sampled; see editorial note 4.]

### 4.1 Document-Grounded Audit

The cross-model panels in Section 4.2 measure how far independent extractions agree with each other. They cannot tell an error that every model shares from the truth. As a complementary check, we audited the released values directly against the source documents. The audit uses the same stratified sample of 200 permits as the release-composition panel (Section 4.2, fifth panel). Those permits were drawn from the release itself, stratified by the model that produced each permit's rows (in proportion to that model's share) and by document-length terciles, with a fixed random seed. Using the same sample means the audit and panel results can be compared permit by permit.

An LLM auditor (Claude, a model that produced no released records) performed the audit with access to the full extracted text of each source document. This is an automated audit, not a human one, and we label it as such. Two design choices make up for that. First, every verdict is tied to the document. For each of ten general fields (facility name, address, city, state, ZIP code, permit number, issuance date, expiration date, regulatory authority, NAICS code) the auditor gives exactly one of five verdicts: correct (the document supports the value; formatting variants count as correct), incorrect (the document clearly states a different value), unsupported (the released value is not null, but nothing in the document supports it), missed (the released value is null, but the document states one), or null-ok (the value is null and the document really does not give it). Every error verdict must include a quoted snippet from the document and the document's value, so each flag can be checked independently. Second, those quotes make human review of the flagged permits cheap. The 44 permits with at least one field error, plus 12 flagged only at the unit level (56 in all), are the priority set for human review, and the repository provides them with their quotes as a review sheet. For emission units, the auditor checked up to six released units per permit (spread across the list) to confirm they exist and, where stated, that their type and capacity match, and ran a bounded recall probe that lists units clearly present in the document but missing from the release. The audit protocol, per-permit results with evidence, and aggregation script are in the repository (`data/processed/validation/manual_audit/`).

Of the 2,000 field-level judgments (10 fields x 200 permits), 1,938 (96.9%) were correct or correctly null. The 62 errors fell in 44 permits: 19 incorrect, 15 unsupported, and 28 missed (Table 3). Facility identity fields were nearly perfect (facility name 100%, state 99.5%, address 98.0%). The weakest fields were permit number (94.0%, where 10 of the 12 errors were cautious nulls rather than wrong values) and issuance date (94.0%). The issuance-date errors follow a pattern: an application, effective, or renewal date taken as the issuance date, or a proposed permit whose issuance field is genuinely blank. This is the same problem of several unlabeled dates that produced the 78% panel agreement on this field in Section 4.2, and it is why we advise users who need exact issuance dates to check the source document. The 15 unsupported values (0.75% of judgments) bound how often the extraction asserts something the document does not contain.

At the unit level, 790 released units were checked. Of these, 764 (96.7%) were found in the source document, 24 (3.0%) exist but with a different type or capacity, and only 2 (0.25%) could not be found at all. Invented equipment records are essentially absent. The recall probe found at least one emission unit listed in the document but missing from the release in 39 of 200 permits (19.5%). Because the probe has a fixed effort budget, that figure is itself a lower bound. It independently confirms the under-counting found in Section 4.2 and the lower-bound reading of unit counts in Section 4.4.

### 4.2 Multi-Model Cross-Validation

As an independent check that scales, the pipeline has a multi-model validation mode. Three models from different providers each extract the same permit text, and their outputs are compared field by field after normalization (case-folding, collapsing whitespace, and parsing numbers). A permit is flagged for review if fewer than two models succeed, if any key general field has conflicting values, or if the unit-level agreement ratio is below 70%. Emission units are matched across models by unit identifier.

We ran this on 202 randomly sampled permits in two panels. Both panels use the same two verifier models (Gemini 2.5 Flash and GLM-5) and differ only in the third model: gpt-oss-120b in one panel (n = 100), and Gemma 4 in the other (n = 102). At the time, gpt-oss-120b had produced 27% of records in the then-current snapshot; it produced 2% of records in this release. Of the 202 permits, 194 gave usable comparisons, meaning at least two models succeeded. An API gateway authorization expired partway through one panel run and at first left 54 permits with fewer than two successful extractions. A follow-up run on the same panel recovered 47 of them. The eight permits that remain unusable are large, unit-heavy documents for which fewer than two models returned complete output within the token and timeout limits.

Among permits where at least one model produced a value, agreement on structured general fields was high: facility state (99%), expiration date (99%), ZIP code (97%), city (97%), facility address (83%), issuance date (81%), permit number (78%), and facility name (76%, where most disagreements were formatting variants such as corporate suffixes). Agreement was lower for fields that permits write in many different ways: permit type (43%), regulatory authority (36%, from variant agency names), and primary applicable regulations (15%, a free-text field where models phrase the same programs differently). At the unit level (1,052 unit-model comparisons), agreement was high for structured attributes, namely capacity value (94%), fuel type (85%), capacity unit (77%), and NESHAP/NSPS subpart citations (69%), and lower for free-text fields: control devices (65%), pollutants (45%), emission limits (40%), and unit description (34%). The per-permit unit agreement ratio averaged 0.75 (median 0.74); 56% of permits met the 0.70 threshold and 23% agreed on every compared unit field. An earlier set of runs with a different verifier panel (GPT-4.1 and Claude Sonnet alongside gpt-oss-120b; n = 61, March 2026) showed closely similar field-level agreement, so the pattern is stable across model panels.

Exact matching on normalized strings counts a paraphrase, an abbreviation, or a reordering as a disagreement. The percentages above therefore understate real agreement, especially for free-text fields. To measure this directly, we added a content-level adjudication step and applied it to a fresh random sample of 100 permits. A third panel extracted them, keeping the two shared verifier models (Gemini 2.5 Flash and GLM-5) and putting Claude Sonnet 4.6 in the third slot. A fourth model that did no extraction (gpt-oss-120b) then reviewed the three extractions for each permit alongside the source text. Field by field, it ruled whether the values agree on content, meaning identical, equivalent (paraphrase, abbreviation, reordering, or unit and format differences), or compatible (one value more complete than another, with no contradiction), or whether they genuinely conflict. For genuine conflicts it recorded which value the source supports. Of the 100 permits, 98 had at least two usable extractions. Content-level agreement on key general fields was 94% (861 of 918 field-level judgments), against 67% (616 of 917) under exact string matching. That 27-percentage-point gap is 244 field judgments that exact matching scored as disagreement but the adjudicator ruled equivalent in content (Table 4). The gap sat in exactly the free-text fields that exact matching handles badly: agreement on primary applicable regulations rose from 5% to 91%, regulatory authority from 24% to 100%, and permit type from 33% to 86%. Tightly formatted fields (facility state, city, ZIP code, and dates) were already near-unanimous and did not change. Emission-unit lists agreed on content for 81 of the 98 permits. Among the 57 field-level disagreements judged genuine conflicts, no single model was consistently right. The source supported Gemini 2.5 Flash most often, but corrections were spread across all three extractors. The remaining conflicts therefore come from ambiguity in particular documents, not from one weaker model.

A fourth panel, run in July 2026, tested the model that now produces most released records. One hundred documents, stratified across document-length quintiles, were extracted by Gemini 2.5 Flash under the chunked scheme and compared with a free-model baseline (gemma-4-thinking) that had already processed the same documents. The same independent adjudicator (gpt-oss-120b) ruled on content agreement. Gemini 2.5 Flash recovered 5,516 emission units against the baseline's 3,552 (a factor of 1.55), with the gain concentrated in the longest documents. Content agreement on key general fields was 95% (967 of 1,019 field-level judgments), and emission-unit lists were judged in full, equivalent, or superset agreement for 69 of 99 documents, with "superset" the most common verdict. Among the 52 genuine field-level conflicts, the source slightly more often favored the baseline model. This is why the merge in Section 2.3 adds rather than replaces: neither model beats the other on every document. A second candidate model (Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite) was tested in the same panel and rejected, because it returned empty emission-unit lists for nearly every document even though it filled the general fields correctly.

A fifth panel, run in August 2026, checks the released dataset itself rather than a re-run of it. Two hundred permits were sampled from the release as it stood on 2026-08-02 (56,749 documents), stratified by the model that produced each permit's rows, in proportion to that model's share of released permits, and by document-length terciles. The panel therefore matches that snapshot's mix (56% Gemini 2.5 Flash, 21% Llama, 20% records with no recorded model string, 2% gpt-oss-120b, 1% Gemini 2.0 Flash-Lite). The documents added after that date (Section 2.3) were all extracted by Gemini 2.5 Flash under the chunked scheme, which is the largest stratum of this panel. The released extraction, rebuilt from the released rows, was compared with independent re-extractions by Claude Sonnet and GLM-5, neither of which produced any released records. A fourth model that did no extraction judged content agreement against the source text. All 200 permits yielded at least two usable extractions.

Content agreement on key general fields was 95% (1,826 of 1,923 field-level judgments). Every identity and permit-identification field was at or above 94%: facility address (100%), state (99%), regulatory authority (99%), ZIP code (99%), city (98%), facility name (97%), permit number (95%), and expiration date (94%). Issuance date (78%) and primary applicable regulations (89%) were lower. Issuance date is the weakest field in the panel. Permits often carry several dates (signature, effective, renewal) without consistent labels, and users who need exact issuance dates should check the source document. Emission-unit lists agreed on content for 80% of permits (159 of 200: superset 46%, content 22%, full 12%), leaving 20% with genuine conflicts. By released model, unit-list agreement was 88% for permits produced by Llama, 80% for the group with no recorded model, and 77% for Gemini 2.5 Flash.

Of the 97 field-level judgments the adjudicator ruled genuine conflicts, the source supported the released value in 43 cases and an independent extractor in 61. Conflicts are 5% of all judgments and the counts are small. Even so, they give no reason to treat the released model as the most accurate of the three. They confirm that the remaining disagreement comes from ambiguity in particular documents, and that merges must keep adding rather than replacing.

Two points about this comparison. First, the exact-match statistics reported above for the first two panels use exact matching on normalized strings. As the content-level adjudication shows, disagreement on free-text fields is mostly a matter of wording (paraphrase, ordering, abbreviation) rather than fact, so the exact-match percentages are conservative lower bounds on cross-model agreement. The document-grounded audit (Section 4.1) checks meaning against the source directly. Second, because units are matched by unit identifier, agreement on unit ID is perfect by construction and is left out of the reported metrics. Under the strict review gate, which flags a permit if any single key field disagrees, 187 of 194 usable permits were flagged, almost entirely because of the free-text regulations, permit-type, and regulatory-authority fields. As the adjudication results show, these flags reflect wording far more than real error. The gate is deliberately conservative: it is a triage tool, not an accuracy estimate.

### 4.3 Automated Quality Control

We ran automated checks on the full dataset (1,621,542 rows; 1,620,837 successful). The QC script and its machine-readable report are in the repository.

- **Format validation**: among successful rows with a value, 100.0% of state abbreviations are valid two-letter U.S. codes and 99.99% of ZIP codes are five or nine digits. Issuance and expiration dates are in ISO 8601 format in 99.5% and 99.4% of cases, up from 69.1% and 64.4% in earlier releases, after re-extraction with an enforced response schema.
- **Completeness profiling**: how much is missing varies widely by field and reflects what permits actually report. Facility name and state are almost always present. Generation capacity (97.0% missing), rated efficiency (93.4%), and annual run hours (79.7%) are sparse because most permits do not state them for each unit. All three improved on earlier releases as chunked re-extraction recovered unit detail from long documents.
- **Cross-field consistency**: 99.7% of the 884,782 rows with a capacity value also carry a capacity unit, up from 92.3%.
- **Duplicate detection**: 2,976 of the 1,488,473 unit rows (0.2%) share a (source document, unit identifier) key with another row, in 1,053 groups. This happens where a permit reuses an identifier across separate process lines, or where merged chunk extractions gave the same identifier to units the deduplication key could not tell apart. On the (Filename, Unit ID, Unit Description) triple the residue falls to 1,126 rows (0.08%). Users who need strictly unique unit keys should deduplicate on that triple. No "ERROR" sentinel values appear in successful rows.

### 4.4 Known Error Modes

Users should be aware of several systematic error modes:

- **OCR artifacts**: scanned permits can produce swapped characters in unit IDs, capacity values, or pollutant abbreviations (for example, "NOx" read as "N0x").
- **Unit boundary ambiguity**: some permits put several pieces of equipment under one unit ID, or describe unit attributes in running text rather than tables. Either can lead to under- or over-counting of distinct emission units. The small residue of duplicated unit identifiers within a document (0.2% of unit rows) comes from this ambiguity.
- **Residual unit under-counting**: earlier releases missed many emission units in long permits. Chunked re-extraction of every long document (Section 2.3) helped a great deal. Total unit records roughly quadrupled, and the drop in unit density with document length is much smaller. But it did not remove the problem, and **unit counts should still be read as lower bounds**. In the validation panel of Section 4.2, an independent extractor found more units than the release on a large minority of permits. Across the 195 permits processed by all three panel models, the release held 4,805 units against 5,213 for the strongest independent extractor, and the release matched or beat both independents on only 68% of permits. On permits whose rows came from the current primary model, the release had 0.86 of the best independent count. This is the same behavior that motivates additive merging (Section 2.3): no single pass finds every unit, and repeated passes keep adding. The document-grounded audit confirms this without relying on any extractor: its bounded recall probe found at least one unit listed in the document but absent from the release in 39 of 200 audited permits (Section 4.1). Documents with a non-zero `Pieces Failed` value (2,333 documents, 3.2%) are incomplete for a separate reason: one or more of their chunks failed even after retries.
- **Limit representation**: permits often write emission limits with conditions, averaging periods, or several clauses. These are kept as free text and may need domain-specific parsing before they can be used as numbers.
- **Address disambiguation**: the LLM sometimes picks a corporate or mailing address instead of the facility's physical address, especially when the permit lists several addresses without clear labels.
- **Temporal coverage**: the dataset reflects the permits available when we collected them and may include expired permits alongside current ones. Filter on issuance and expiration dates for analyses that depend on time.

## 5. Usage Notes

### 5.1 Recommended Filtering

For analysis, keep records whose `Status` is "Success" (or also "Success (No Units Found)" for permit-level analyses). Keep the failure rows when measuring coverage or finding documents to review or reprocess.

### 5.2 Industry Classification

Industry classification is given as a resolved code with a stated source. Permits state industry codes inconsistently. A NAICS code appears in only 20% of successfully extracted permits and a SIC code in 30%, and the codes that do appear are not always clean: 10.2% of filled NAICS values start with a two-digit sector that does not exist in NAICS, because some agencies put SIC codes in a generically labeled field, and some values are semicolon-joined lists of several codes.

The pipeline therefore picks one `NAICS Resolved` code per permit from the best evidence available, in a fixed order of preference, and records which source supplied it in `NAICS Source`, so users can limit their analysis to the sources they trust. The order is: a valid six-digit code stated in the permit itself (15.4% of permits); the NAICS code that EPA ECHO records for the facility the permit matched in the coverage benchmark of Section 3.4, including matches made through the source agency's registry name (35.2%); the pipeline's own text-derived classification, with catch-all codes excluded (10.9%); a two-digit sector converted from a SIC code stated in the permit (8.6%); and last, an LLM classification from the facility name, industry description, and a sample permitted unit, which must return no answer rather than guess when the text is uninformative (20.9%). Multi-code values are split and the first valid code kept. Values whose sector does not exist in NAICS are rejected from the NAICS path and sent through the SIC conversion instead.

This gives a code to 58,987 of the 64,753 successfully extracted permits (91.1%), against 20% from permit-stated codes alone. The remaining 8.9% are mostly documents added in September 2026, for which the LLM stage has not yet run [NOTE: see editorial note 6; on the August snapshot the same procedure resolved 99.3%]. The scheme also removes a systematic bias. The pipeline's earlier text-derived field assigned `339999` ("All Other Miscellaneous Manufacturing") to 13.5% of classified permits, overriding more specific codes that were demonstrably right, and it agreed with a permit-stated six-digit code only 43% of the time when both were present. Under the resolved scheme, `339999` is excluded from the derived path and assigned to 33 permits.

The resolution runs only on permits with at least one successfully extracted row, because it draws on fields that failed extractions do not fill. The 9,264 released documents with no such row (9,253 permits from which no emission units were extracted, plus the failures) therefore have empty `NAICS Resolved` and `NAICS Source` values, and a resolved code is present for 79.7% of the 74,017 released documents. Users doing permit-level analyses that include `Success (No Units Found)` records, as Section 5.1 allows, should expect that gap and fall back to the raw `NAICS Code` and `SIC Code` fields for those documents. Users who want only agency-stated classifications should keep `NAICS Source` values of `permit` or `echo`. The `llm` source is a model's inference and should be treated as one.

Industry totals should be computed over facilities, not permit documents. The number of documents per facility varies by more than tenfold across sources, because some agencies publish one document per facility and others one per permitted device. In the most extreme case, a single air district contributes 6,478 permit documents for 104 facilities, or 62 documents per facility, against 1.3 to 2.0 for most sources. That district would swamp any sector count weighted by documents.

### 5.3 Permit Program Scope

The pipeline targets Title V operating permits, but some agency portals store other permit types alongside them (state-only operating permits, synthetic minor permits, and construction or PSD permits), and some downloaded documents belong to those types. The data-center lanes in Section 2.1 collect construction and minor-source permits on purpose, because much of the on-site generation at those facilities is permitted outside Title V. Their 4,811 documents can be identified by the `Source Run` values `dc_generators_2026-08`, `gcp_dclane2_2026-09`, and `gcp_dclane3_2026-09`. The `Primary Applicable Regulations` field records the regulatory programs each permit cites. Users who want only Title V sources should filter on this field, keeping in mind that it reflects the permit's wording rather than a verified regulatory classification.

### 5.4 Emission Limits

Emission limits are kept as free text, because permits often state them in complex, conditional forms (rolling averages, startup and shutdown exceptions, limits that depend on the fuel). Several limits for one unit are separated by semicolons. Users who need numeric limits should parse them in a downstream step suited to their domain. Two related limits have their own columns where the permit states them: `Opacity Limit` (15% of successful records) and `Throughput/Production Limit` (17%). Both are naturally sparse, since they apply only to the kinds of equipment for which such limits are written, and both are free text.

### 5.5 Linking to External Data

The `Facility Name`, `Owner/Operator Name`, `Facility Address`, `Permit Number`, and industry-classification columns can link records to outside registries such as EPA's ECHO database, the Facility Registry Service, the National Emissions Inventory, and the Toxics Release Inventory. Identifiers vary across sources, so users should use fuzzy matching, or authoritative facility identifiers (such as the EPA FRS ID) where they exist. The ECHO matching procedure behind the coverage statistics in Section 3.4 (exact matching on standardized names within a state, then guarded fuzzy matching, with the source agency's registry names as aliases) is implemented in the repository's benchmark and alias scripts and can serve as a reference. That match has already been run: permits whose `NAICS Source` is `echo` are the ones where it succeeded, so the field also shows which records have a confirmed ECHO counterpart. Note that `Owner/Operator Name` often names a parent company while `Facility Name` names the site, so the two link to different entities in analyses of corporate structure.

### 5.6 Unit-Level Interpretation

Each unit row is an emission unit as the permit describes it, but unit definitions and identifiers are not standard across jurisdictions. Some permits group several pieces of equipment under one unit ID, and others list control devices as separate units. Unit counts are therefore what the permit reports, not an inventory of physical equipment. A permit's units may come from several extraction passes (Section 2.3). Counts rose substantially in this release, so users comparing unit counts across releases should recompute their totals rather than reuse cached ones. For analyses that depend on completeness, exclude documents with a non-zero `Pieces Failed` value.

### 5.7 Example Application: On-Site Generation at Data Centers

To show a unit-level question that no existing database answers, the repository includes a screen of permitted on-site electricity generation at data centers, built entirely on this dataset. Documents are ranked by how firmly they can be tied to a data center (by facility name, industry code, acquisition lane, or verified permit text). Documents that describe the same site are grouped by agency facility identifier, permit number, street address, and city plus a distinctive word in the name. Generator units are then deduplicated across a site's documents, which removes restated unit lists, identical fleets described in several documents of one permit, and the same engine reported in both horsepower and kilowatts. On the current release the screen finds 911 candidate sites, 636 of them firmly attributed, with a combined permitted generating capacity of about 103 GW (27 GW prime or non-emergency, 76 GW emergency backup). These totals are floors, because a large minority of generator units are permitted by count rather than rating. They are permitted capacity, not installed capacity. The builder script and the resulting site and unit tables are in the repository, and the method is described in the script's documentation. [NOTE: optional section; see editorial note 10.]

## 6. Code Availability

All code for collecting documents, extracting text, extracting fields with an LLM, and post-processing is in the project's open-source GitHub repository. The repository is an installable Python package (`permit_data_extraction`) with these main parts:

- **Acquisition tools**: the agentic portal crawler (`permit_data_extraction/pdf_downloader.py`), the EPA permit hub downloader, and 55 state-specific download scripts (under `scripts/`), covering EPA and roughly 40 state and local agency portals.
- **Text extraction**: the pipeline entry point (`run_extraction_pipeline.py`), with native PDF parsing and OCR fallback.
- **Information extraction**: the LLM extraction module (`permit_data_extraction/dataset.py`), which holds the prompt template, schema definitions, post-processing logic, and output consolidation.
- **Benchmarking and classification**: the ECHO coverage benchmark and source-agency alias builder (Section 3.4), the industry-classification resolution scripts (Section 5.2), and the automated QC script (Section 4.3).
- **Testing and validation**: test suites for data generation and extraction quality, the validation-panel and audit tools of Section 4, and the data-center generation screen of Section 5.7.

The repository README lists the dependencies (Python packages, Poppler, and Tesseract for OCR), the environment setup (API keys for LLM access), and the commands to reproduce each pipeline stage. A release tag matching the dataset version described in this paper is archived with a persistent identifier for long-term preservation.
